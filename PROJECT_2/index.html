Project 1: Colorizing the Prokudin-Gorskii Photo Collection

CS180 · Saksham

Overview

The goal of this project was to fundamentally understand and emulate how photography was traditionally stitched together to form colored images some time ago. Using most of numpy and scikit/sckit.io I was able to convert three discrete still exposure taken back to back into three color channels and align them together to form the target colored image. The reason why there was a need for allignment is because there might be subtle perturbations between these image shots such as brightness change or slight movements which might need to be alligned precisely for the purpose of overlapping the color channels. For which I used a brute force search on the displacement vector for smaller sized images, and ran a recursive search of increasingly downscaled pixels for bigger images.

Approach

For JPEGs brute force across a 30 width search on both the displacement length and height, using np.roll mechanic to simulate that and trying both the L2 Norm and the NCC as loss functions. A lot of interesting observations.

First pass

My first real cathedral result was (-1, 1) and (-1, 8). Now it's (2, 5) and (3, 11). I ran print statements across the loss function output and realized the problem was the plate borders. The black frame and bright scanning margins are high contrast, and they sit in the same place in all three chunks no matter how the actual photo is offset. L2 tried to match them and got pulled toward zero. Once I stopped scoring on the borders, cathedral snapped to (5, 2) and (11, 3).

Interesting observation

np.roll wraps around the last few pixels back to the start. If you shift a channel down 10 rows, the bottom 10 rows reappear at the top. That is actually real photo content, but from the wrong place, so my variance-based border detection walked straight past it. You can see it in some of the outputs as a colored strip on one edge. I ended up cropping after alignment as well as before, but it's the thing I'd most want to handle properly with more time.

Pyramid trouble

I got the recursion backwards twice. First I was calling rescale with a factor of 2 instead of 0.5, so the images got bigger going down. Then I was doubling the center on the way down instead of on the way up. The way it should work: shrink until the image is small, search wide there, and then on the way back up double the child's estimate and refine it with a small window around that. The base case should be about image size, not window size.

NCC bug

My NCC scored cathedral's red channel at (8, 3) with 0.7524 and the correct (11, 3) with 0.7453. I printed every score in the window and the surface was a smooth dome, not a sharp peak. That means the metric was correlating the low-frequency stuff, the sky gradient and vignetting, instead of the cathedral's edges. Restricting the comparison to the middle 60% of the image, where the vignetting isn't, fixed it.

Speedup

My first run on a tif took 86 seconds. Two Python loops inside the search, one rolling row by row and one summing per-row norms, were running thousands of times per candidate shift. Replacing them with a single np.roll on both axes and a single np.sum brought it down to a few seconds. The spec says aim for under a minute per image and it wasn't kidding.

Where it failed

The Emir's robe is deep blue, so it comes out bright through the blue filter and almost black through the red one. My metric compares brightness, and for the robe the brightness just doesn't match at any shift. So the search lines up the plain wall behind him instead and the robe ends up off. Matching on edges instead of raw brightness would fix this, since the outline of the robe is in the same place in all three channels no matter how bright it is.

Bells and whistles

Variance based automatic cropping

My approach to removing the borders was that I used the fact that these borders are low contrast horizontally/vertically. So I computed the variance of every row and column, set a threshold relative to the image's own mean variance, and scanned inward from each edge until I hit a line above threshold. My first version deleted every low-variance row anywhere in the image, which would have shredded the sky. Borders have to be contiguous with the edge, so the scan has to stop.
